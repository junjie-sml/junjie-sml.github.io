About / Research

My research seeks a mechanistic understanding of how molecular programs, cells, and tissues organize and communicate. I pursue this through interpretable statistical modeling and graph-based machine learning across single-cell and spatial omics.

1. Graphical models and gene regulatory inference in scRNA-seq data

Single-cell RNA-seq provides a discrete, sparse, and heterogeneous view of gene expression at cellular resolution, yet the biological questions hinge on identifying direct regulatory dependencies rather than broad associations. My work focuses on principled graphical formulations that are faithful to count-based sampling while remaining scalable in high dimensions and robust to mixed cell populations. By aligning model structure with the statistics of single-cell data, these approaches recover precision-matrix structure that reflects putative regulatory interactions across and within cell types, enabling clearer separation of direct from indirect effects and offering a more reliable substrate for downstream network biology.

2. Super-resolution interpretable embedding learning for spatial transcriptomics

Spatial transcriptomics links gene expression to tissue architecture, but practical measurements suffer from limited spatial resolution and substantial noise. I develop embedding formulations that preserve spatial coherence and biological meaning while enhancing effective resolution. The goal is not only to produce compact representations, but to make the “axes” of the embedding legible—connecting each latent dimension to interpretable gene programs and spatial patterns that refine tissue micro-organization. This perspective turns dimensionality reduction into a tool for reading out biologically grounded structure from noisy spatial assays and diverse histology contexts.

3. Intercellular functional interaction module inference for spatial transcriptomics

Multicellular behavior emerges from coordinated, directional communication that extends far beyond isolated ligand–receptor pairs. I study cell–cell signaling as modules that link upstream regulators, signaling bridges, and downstream targets into compact programs that act across space and across cell types. Modeling communication in this modular, pathway-aligned way reveals multi-hop cascades, clarifies how local exchanges scale to tissue-level phenotypes, and supports hypothesis-driven comparisons and in silico perturbations. The emphasis is on interpretability and directional structure, so that inferred modules can be read as mechanistic hypotheses about how tissues change state in development and disease.
